# Part I — Six additions for AKAsheet

## 1. “Day One” fan identity

When someone buys an early First Drop, make them visibly part of that song’s history:

- “Day One #7” supporter badge
- Purchase timestamp and release edition
- Artist thank-you message
- Private progress updates
- Early access to revisions or the completed version
- Supporter displayed on the song page if they opt in
- Notification when the song reaches milestones

Keep it a collectible supporter receipt—not financial ownership or crypto.

This turns buying from a plain transaction into status and emotional attachment.

## 2. Pass the Bar

Make collaboration immediate:

- Artist records or writes one bar.
- Challenges a friend to respond.
- Friend opens a playable link.
- Friend submits the next bar.
- The exchange becomes a shareable vertical clip.
- Either person can convert it into a Collab Sheet.

This is the kind of activity that naturally recruits another person every time it is used.

## 3. Professional split sheets

Give collaborators something genuinely valuable:

- Songwriter names and roles
- Proposed ownership percentages
- Contribution history
- Approval from each collaborator
- Downloadable dated PDF
- Track version and lyric snapshot

Do not advertise it as legal copyright registration, but make it a clean working record. “Write it, agree on it, preserve it” could become a major reason professionals use BA.

## 4. Automatic social exports

Every House Bar, First Drop, lyric section, and collaboration should produce a polished:

- 9:16 video
- Audio waveform preview
- Animated lyrics
- Artist branding
- Deep link back to the playable item
- “Reply with a bar” or “Support this drop” CTA

TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube should be BA’s distribution channels. The content travels outward, while participation happens inside BA.

## 5. Real artist analytics

Show artists information they can act on:

- Who listened
- Preview completion rate
- Shares and referral origin
- Replies generated
- Purchases and conversion rate
- Returning supporters
- Top cities
- Which lyric or preview caused the most engagement
- Sales, fees, artist net, and payout status

Do not drown them in charts. Tell them things such as: “18 people finished your preview; 4 bought it; most arrived from Jasmine’s share.”

## 6. A weekly cultural event

Make the House Bar more than a widget:

- One major prompt every week
- Genre-specific rooms
- Community voting plus a craft score
- Featured winner
- Live finale or listening room
- Winner receives homepage placement, Ink, or a fee-free First Drop day
- Shareable winner cards

You need moments when users know everyone else will be present.

# Part II — Companies and organizations likely to be interested

> Original question: **“Who/what are some companies that I am overlooking who will 1000% be interested in this app?”**

Nobody is guaranteed to be interested, but some organizations are unusually strong fits because BA can solve an existing business problem for them.

The two most overlooked targets are not record labels: they’re songwriter-rights organizations and music-education programs.

## The most strategically interesting company: EVEN

EVEN is both a potential partner and a competitor.

It already provides:

- Sell-before-streaming releases
- Fan data
- Revenue splits
- Daily payouts
- Chart reporting
- Exclusive content and supporter experiences
- Stripe Connect infrastructure

It has recently announced partnerships with UnitedMasters, Too Lost, Symphonic, Secretly Distribution, Cinq, and UMG. That proves it is willing to integrate with other artist platforms. [EVEN partnership announcements](https://backstage.even.biz/news)

Your pitch should not be “BA also sells music.” It should be:

> “EVEN begins when an artist has a release. BA begins while the song is still being written. We can deliver release-ready artists, complete collaborator information, early fan demand, and creation history into EVEN.”

That is a legitimate strategic fit.

EVEN’s terms also reveal that direct music sales eventually involve licensing, collaborator splits, sales reporting, taxes, refunds, and rights responsibilities—not merely Stripe checkout. Partnering with established release infrastructure might ultimately be safer than making BA recreate every piece itself. [EVEN terms](https://get.even.biz/terms-of-use)

# Part III — Fan resale with recurring artist royalties

> Original question: **“What about fan resale of a song while the artist always receives a percentage off every sale? A fan sends/sells a song and no longer has it—what do you think with our current structure?”**

I think this could become one of AKAsheet’s strongest differentiators—but it should be framed as an artist-authorized transfer of a limited “First Drop Edition,” not as reselling ownership of the song itself.

Right now, BA is not structured for resale:

- A purchase grants one permanent library entitlement associated with the song/share.
- Purchases are not numbered or individually owned editions.
- Checkout sends the artist’s share to one Stripe destination.
- The current Terms explicitly describe the license as non-transferable and prohibit resale: [terms/page.tsx](</G:/AKAsheet App $/AKAsheet FULL APP 5-28-2026 YOYO SAVE VER56/src/app/terms/page.tsx:60>).
- Full playback is controlled server-side, which is a major advantage: BA can revoke the seller’s stream access and grant it to the recipient.

## The version I would build

Call it something like **Pass the Drop** or **First Drop Editions**.

1. The artist chooses whether a release is transferable.
2. They issue a limited number—for example, 100 numbered editions.
3. Each edition has one current holder.
4. The holder can:

   - Gift it to another BA user.
   - List it for resale.
5. Once accepted or sold, the previous holder loses full-song access and edition perks.
6. The artist receives an automatic royalty from every paid resale.
7. The edition keeps a visible history: original buyer, transfers, resale prices and current holder.

No blockchain or NFT is necessary. Firestore can provide the ownership ledger.

The important limitation is that “you no longer have it” can only mean the seller loses access inside BA. No service can guarantee they never recorded the audio externally.

## Why limited editions matter

If the artist continues selling unlimited identical access, buyers have little reason to purchase from another fan. Resale becomes meaningful when the edition includes scarcity or status:

- “Edition 17 of 100”
- Day-one supporter badge
- Original release artwork
- Holder-only updates or alternate versions
- Early access to the artist’s next drop
- Public provenance/history

The music remains the artist’s property. The fan owns only the transferable in-app access edition.

## Recommended royalty structure

I would begin with:

- Artist royalty: **15% of every resale**
- Seller: approximately **60–70%**
- BA: the remainder, including enough to cover Stripe processing, fraud and marketplace costs

The artist’s royalty should be locked when editions are issued, so a future owner knows the resale terms cannot suddenly change.

I would not reuse the present 10% fee at higher prices without modeling it carefully. At $5, 10% gives BA only $0.50 before card processing—almost the entire platform portion could disappear. This is a more expensive transaction than the current primary sale because two people may need payouts.

Stripe can split one payment among multiple connected accounts using separate charges and transfers, but BA would be responsible for payment fees, refunds and chargebacks in that structure. Anyone receiving cash—including a fan-reseller—would generally need Stripe Connect onboarding and verification. [Stripe’s marketplace payment documentation](https://docs.stripe.com/connect/separate-charges-and-transfers), [seller verification requirements](https://docs.stripe.com/connect/handle-verification-updates).

## Legal distinction

BA should not rely on the normal “first sale doctrine” for digital files. U.S. authorities have rejected the assumption that sending a digital file and deleting the old one automatically creates a lawful resale, and the ReDigi case found infringement where digital music was reproduced during resale. [U.S. Copyright Office discussion](https://www.copyright.gov/reports/annual/2001/ar2001.pdf), [Capitol Records v. ReDigi](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/16-2321/16-2321-2018-12-12.html).

BA has a better route: the artist expressly authorizes a transferable in-app license when pressing the edition. That needs revised Terms and a music/IP lawyer’s review, especially where songs contain leased beats, samples, co-writers or label-controlled recordings.

## The safest rollout

1. **Gift transfers first:** “Pass this Drop.” No money, recipient gains access, sender loses access. This tests whether people actually value transferability.
2. **Limited numbered editions:** artist opt-in, fixed resale royalty and visible ownership history.
3. **Paid resale marketplace:** Stripe onboarding for sellers, atomic ownership transfers, payout holds, fraud handling and resale earnings reporting.

The gifting version could create a strong viral loop immediately: every transferred song brings another fan into BA. Then paid resale adds recurring artist income once the ownership, legal and payout infrastructure is ready.

I made no code changes while evaluating this.
